4.2 Section B marking guidance

4.3 (a) Define scarcity. [2]

4.3.1 Full-mark answer

Scarcity exists because resources are limited in relation to unlimited human wants. Therefore, it is impossible to produce all the goods and services that people would like to consume, so choices have to be made.

4.3.2 Why this earns full marks

  • 1 mark: identifies that resources are limited.
  • 1 mark: links limited resources to unlimited wants and the need for choice.

A definition that states only that resources are limited is incomplete because it does not establish the relationship between limited resources and unlimited wants.

Key terms: limited resources; unlimited wants; choice.

4.4 (b) Explain two reasons why the production of electric scooters involves an opportunity cost. [4]

4.4.1 Full-mark answer

The production of electric scooters uses scarce lithium batteries, specialised machinery, skilled labour and finance. These resources could have been used to produce alternative goods and services, such as electric bicycles, public transport equipment or other manufactured products. The value of the best alternative that is sacrificed is the opportunity cost of producing the scooters.

The government may also use public funds to support scooter production or consumption through subsidies, infrastructure or research grants. These funds could instead be spent on schools, hospitals, public transport or other environmental projects. The benefit lost from the best alternative use of these funds is another opportunity cost.

4.4.2 Why this earns full marks

The question requires two explained reasons, not merely two examples of scarce resources.

Reason Economic development Marks
Use of productive resources Lithium, machinery, skilled labour and finance used for scooters cannot simultaneously be used for their next-best alternative use. 2
Use of government resources Subsidies, infrastructure spending or research support may displace spending on education, healthcare, public transport or other environmental projects. 2

For each reason:

  • 1 mark: identifies a relevant scarce resource or alternative use.
  • 1 mark: explains that the next-best alternative is forgone and therefore represents opportunity cost.

The answer is applied to the context through lithium batteries, specialised machinery, skilled labour, subsidies and public infrastructure.

4.5 (c) Calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded. [6]

4.5.1 1. Price of scooters increases by 8\%

The formula is:

\text{PED} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_d}{\%\Delta P}

Therefore:

\%\Delta Q_d = \text{PED} \times \%\Delta P

\%\Delta Q_d = -1.5 \times 8\% = -12\%

Quantity demanded decreases by 12\%.

4.5.2 2. Consumer incomes increase by 5\%

The formula is:

\text{YED} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_d}{\%\Delta Y}

Therefore:

\%\Delta Q_d = \text{YED} \times \%\Delta Y

\%\Delta Q_d = +2.0 \times 5\% = +10\%

Quantity demanded increases by 10\%.

4.5.3 3. Price of electric bicycles falls by 10\%

The formula is:

\text{XED} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_d\text{ of scooters}}{\%\Delta P\text{ of electric bicycles}}

Therefore:

\%\Delta Q_d\text{ of scooters} = \text{XED} \times \%\Delta P\text{ of electric bicycles}

\%\Delta Q_d = -0.4 \times (-10\%) = +4\%

Demand for electric scooters increases by 4\%.

4.5.4 Final answers

Change Elasticity Calculation Effect on quantity demanded
Scooter price increases by 8\% \text{PED} = -1.5 -1.5 \times 8\% Decreases by 12\%
Income increases by 5\% \text{YED} = +2.0 +2.0 \times 5\% Increases by 10\%
Electric bicycle price falls by 10\% \text{XED} = -0.4 -0.4 \times -10\% Increases by 4\%

4.5.5 Why this earns full marks

Award 2 marks for each calculation:

  • 1 mark: correct elasticity relationship and substitution.
  • 1 mark: correct numerical answer and direction of change.

The signs must be interpreted correctly. The negative PED means that quantity demanded changes in the opposite direction to price. The positive YED indicates that electric scooters are a normal good, and the negative XED indicates that electric scooters and electric bicycles are substitutes.

4.6 (d) Explain two factors that may determine the price elasticity of supply in the short run. [4]

4.6.1 Full-mark answer

First, the availability of spare capacity affects the price elasticity of supply. The manufacturer has limited spare capacity, so it may be unable to increase output substantially when the price rises. Existing machinery and workers may already be fully used, making supply relatively price inelastic in the short run.

Second, the availability of imported components and skilled labour affects supply elasticity. If batteries and other components take time to import, or if specially trained workers are difficult to recruit, the firm cannot respond quickly to a price increase. Quantity supplied will therefore rise by a smaller percentage than price, making short-run supply relatively inelastic.

The time period itself is also important. In the short run, at least one factor of production is fixed, whereas in the long run the firm may expand its factory, purchase machinery, train workers and arrange alternative suppliers. Supply is therefore likely to become more elastic over time.

4.6.2 Why this earns full marks

Factor Explanation Marks
Limited spare capacity Existing machinery and labour are heavily used, so the firm cannot increase output greatly after a price rise. 2
Imported components and skilled labour Delays in obtaining batteries or recruiting trained workers restrict the short-run response of output. 2

For each factor:

  • 1 mark: identifies the factor.
  • 1 mark: explains how it affects the responsiveness of quantity supplied to price.

The answer gains additional quality through direct application to the information in the case: limited spare capacity, imported components and specially trained workers.

4.7 (e) Analyse the likely effect of an 8\% increase in the price of scooters on total revenue. [4]

4.7.1 Full-mark answer

The initial total revenue is:

\text{TR} = P \times Q

\text{TR} = \$500 \times 20,000 = \$10,000,000

The price elasticity of demand is -1.5. Therefore, an 8\% increase in price causes a:

-1.5 \times 8\% = -12\%

fall in quantity demanded.

The new price is:

\$500 \times 1.08 = \$540

The new quantity demanded is:

20,000 \times 0.88 = 17,600

The new total revenue is:

\text{TR} = \$540 \times 17,600 = \$9,504,000

Therefore, total revenue falls by:

\$10,000,000 - \$9,504,000 = \$496,000

The percentage fall in quantity demanded is greater than the percentage increase in price because demand is price elastic. Consequently, the fall in quantity sold outweighs the higher price per scooter, so total revenue decreases.

4.7.2 Why this earns full marks

Required element Evidence in the answer Marks
Initial total revenue \$500 \times 20,000 = \$10,000,000 1
Elasticity analysis An 8\% price increase causes a 12\% fall in quantity demanded 1
New total revenue \$540 \times 17,600 = \$9,504,000 1
Economic conclusion Revenue falls by \$496,000 because demand is elastic 1

A response that merely states “revenue falls because demand is elastic” shows some understanding but does not fully analyse the numerical effect. The calculations are needed to demonstrate the size of the change.

4.8 (f) Discuss whether the government should encourage the production and consumption of electric scooters. [5]

4.8.1 Full-mark answer

The government may have a case for encouraging electric scooters because they may generate positive external benefits. If consumers switch from petrol-powered vehicles to electric scooters, fossil-fuel use and possibly air pollution may fall. They may also reduce road congestion if they replace car journeys. These benefits to third parties are not fully reflected in the market price, so the market may produce and consume fewer electric scooters than is socially optimal. Government support could therefore increase consumption towards the socially efficient level.

Encouragement may also support the development of a new industry. Subsidies, investment in charging infrastructure, or support for research and development could reduce firms’ costs and increase supply. This may create employment, develop technical skills and encourage innovation in battery and transport technology. However, the opportunity cost must be considered: resources used for lithium batteries, specialised machinery, skilled labour and government subsidies cannot be used for other goods and services.

There are also possible disadvantages. Battery production requires scarce resources and may create environmental costs during the extraction of lithium and the manufacture and disposal of batteries. Electric scooters may not always replace car journeys; some users may otherwise have walked or used public transport. In that case, the reduction in congestion and pollution may be smaller than expected. Increased scooter use may also create safety problems, impose costs on pedestrians and require additional public regulation and infrastructure.

Whether government encouragement is justified depends on the size of the external benefits compared with these costs. The government should not provide unlimited support simply because scooters are electric. A targeted policy, such as temporary subsidies for cleaner production combined with safety rules, recycling requirements and investment in appropriate infrastructure, is more likely to be justified. Overall, the government should encourage electric scooters if reliable evidence shows that their external benefits are significant and exceed the environmental, safety and opportunity costs; otherwise, resources may be allocated inefficiently.

4.8.2 Why this earns full marks

This is a short 5-mark discuss question. A full-mark answer needs relevant economic analysis on both sides and a supported judgement. It should remain focused on whether government encouragement is justified in the market for electric scooters.

Mark Evidence in the model answer Reason for awarding the mark
1 “They may generate positive external benefits.” Identifies a relevant market-failure argument and applies it to pollution and congestion.
1 “The market may produce and consume fewer electric scooters than is socially optimal.” Develops the positive-externality argument by explaining why underconsumption may occur.
1 “The opportunity cost must be considered.” Recognises that lithium, machinery, skilled labour, finance and government funds have alternative uses.
1 “Battery production… may create environmental costs… [and] increased scooter use may also create safety problems.” Gives relevant disadvantages, including negative externalities and social costs.
1 “The government should encourage electric scooters if… benefits are significant and exceed… costs.” Provides a balanced, conditional judgement rather than an unsupported opinion.

4.8.3 Analytical chains in the answer

  • Positive externalities → social benefits exceed private benefits → market demand is lower than socially desirable → underconsumption may occur → government intervention may improve resource allocation.
  • Government support → lower costs or improved infrastructure → supply and/or demand increases → output and consumption rise → employment, innovation and environmental benefits may increase.
  • Scarce resources and battery production → opportunity costs and possible environmental damage → social costs may rise → unlimited encouragement may cause over-allocation of resources.
  • Effective policy depends on the size of benefits and costs → evidence and appropriate regulation matter → a targeted policy is preferable to unconditional support.

4.8.4 Why weaker answers lose marks

  • They state that electric scooters are environmentally friendly without explaining the economic implications.
  • They list advantages only and do not discuss opportunity cost, environmental costs or safety concerns.
  • They say that “the government should intervene” without identifying the market failure.
  • They claim that scooters always reduce pollution or congestion without considering whether they replace car journeys, walking or public transport.
  • They provide a conclusion that is not supported by the analysis.

4.8.5 Examiner-style level guidance

  • 5 marks: balanced and relevant discussion, with clear analysis and a supported judgement.
  • 4 marks: both sides are considered and the analysis is mostly relevant, but the judgement is weak, brief or insufficiently supported.
  • 2–3 marks: some relevant knowledge and analysis, but discussion is one-sided, undeveloped or insufficiently applied.
  • 1 mark: a basic relevant point with little or no development.
  • 0 marks: no relevant economic material.

6 General examiner principles

Credit any alternative answer that is economically accurate, relevant to electric scooters and sufficiently developed. Do not require the exact wording used in the model answers.

For explanation questions, distinguish between a stated point and a developed chain of reasoning. A point such as “there are imported components” should not receive full credit unless the student explains how this restricts the short-run supply response.

For numerical questions, award method marks when the correct formula and substitution are shown. Minor arithmetic or rounding errors should not automatically remove marks if the economic method is correct.

For discussion questions, reward application to the case study, balanced analysis, relevant evaluation and a supported judgement. A conclusion alone is not evaluation; it must follow from the analysis of benefits, costs and conditions.
